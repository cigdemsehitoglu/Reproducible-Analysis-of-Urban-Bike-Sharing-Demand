Reproducible Analysis of Urban Bike-Sharing Demand

Peak Hours, Weather Effects & the Impact of Missing Data Imputation

Created on: October 7, 2026

1. Introduction

Bike-sharing systems have become a cornerstone of sustainable urban mobility. Understanding demand patterns is essential for operators to optimize fleet distribution, station capacity, and maintenance schedules.

This reproducible report analyzes a simulated but realistic one-month dataset from a mid-sized European city bike-sharing network. We examine:

  • Overall distribution of daily and hourly rentals
  • Peak demand hours and weekday vs weekend differences
  • Relationship between weather conditions and ridership
  • Effect of missing data imputation on summary statistics and patterns
All visualizations are interactive (hover, zoom, pan). The analysis is fully self-contained and can be reproduced from the underlying data generation logic.

3. Distribution of Observed Rental Counts

Before any imputation, we examine the frequency distribution and central tendency of the observed (non-missing) rental counts.

The distribution is right-skewed. Most hours see moderate demand (median around 145 rentals), while a long tail of high-demand hours (commute peaks and sunny weekends) pulls the mean higher.

4. Hourly Demand Patterns

Average rentals by hour of day, separated by weekday and weekend. Clear commute peaks appear on weekdays; weekends show a smoother midday plateau.

Weekday peaks occur at 08:00 and 17:00–18:00. Weekend demand rises later and stays elevated through the afternoon — classic recreational usage.

5. Weather Impact on Ridership

Box plots of rental counts under different weather conditions (observed data only).

Clear and cloudy days show similar medians. Rain reduces demand by ~35%, while storms cut ridership by more than half. Weather is a strong predictor of short-term demand.

6. Effect of Missing Data Imputation

We compare three strategies for handling the 64 missing rental values:

  • Complete-case – simply drop missing hours
  • Mean imputation – replace NA with overall mean
  • Hour-of-day median imputation – replace NA with the median of that hour across the month
Mean imputation slightly inflates the overall mean and reduces variance. Hour-of-day median imputation better preserves the diurnal pattern and is preferred for this type of cyclic data.

8. Conclusions

  • Bike-sharing demand exhibits strong diurnal and weekly seasonality.
  • Weather (especially precipitation) is a major short-term driver of ridership.
  • Simple mean imputation can distort variance and peak patterns; hour-specific median imputation is more appropriate for this cyclic time series.
  • Interactive visualizations help stakeholders quickly identify actionable peaks and weather sensitivity.
This analysis is fully reproducible. The underlying data generation and imputation logic can be inspected and extended for operational forecasting models.
